# Leadership Coaching Question Bank

Organized by theme from John C. Maxwell's "Winning With People" tips. Each entry has: tip reference, question type, and example questions.

---

## Self-Awareness / Inner Work
**Source**: winning-with-people-104

**Core principle**: Relational success starts with self-awareness before it touches others. Work inward to outward.

**Belief questions** (what he actually believes vs. what he says):
- What do you believe is your greatest leadership liability — the thing that feels natural to you but consistently creates friction?
- When someone on your team disagrees with you publicly, what's your first internal reaction — and what do you do with it?
- How do you know when you've misread a room? What's the moment you realize it, and what happens next?

**Practice questions** (how he actually operates):
- Describe the last time you changed your mind about something significant because someone on your team pushed back. What changed your view?
- You lead managers who manage engineers. When was the last time you got feedback that you were surprised by — something you thought was working that wasn't?
- What's one leadership behavior you've had to actively unlearn from your Meta days? What triggered that unlearning?

**Failure mode questions** (tension/gaps):
- You came from a culture where velocity was survival. What's the cost of that velocity habit when you bring it into a relationship that needs more time?
- Most leaders say they're open to feedback. What's one specific thing you do that signals the opposite, even when you believe you're being open?

## Patience / Long-Term Relationship Investment
**Source**: winning-with-people-080

**Core principle**: Patience in relationships is not passive — it's active choice to value long-term over short-term efficiency.

**Belief questions**:
- What's your honest internal bar for "this person is worth investing patience in"? Does it change based on their level, their potential, or something else?
- When you slow down for someone who needs more time, what are you actually betting on?
- Is there anyone on your team right now you've given up on in the "slow" sense — where you've decided they're just not going to get there and you work around them?

**Practice questions**:
- Tell me about a time you were genuinely impatient — not because the person was wrong, but because you felt the clock pressure. What did the impatience cost you?
- You work with both tenured ServiceNow folks and people newer to agentic AI. How do you calibrate your patience differently for those two groups?
- Describe your last 1:1 with someone who was struggling. How much of that conversation was listening vs. solving?

**Failure mode questions**:
- There's a version of "being patient" that's actually avoidance — not addressing something because it's uncomfortable. How do you know the difference?
- Managers often conflate "patience" with "low bar." When has your patience been mistaken for acceptance of underperformance?
- What's the opportunity cost you never calculate — the long-term relationship you didn't build because short-term efficiency won in the moment?

## Partnership / Mutual Growth
**Source**: winning-with-people-100

**Core principle**: Commit to your partner's growth as much as your own. Imbalance eventually collapses.

**Belief questions**:
- Who has grown more from working with you — you or them? Be honest.
- You have a leadership scope that includes managers. What do you actively do to make those managers better leaders, not just better executors?

**Practice questions**:
- Describe a partnership (peer, skip-level, cross-functional) where both people clearly grew. What made that dynamic work?
- When was the last time you referred a peer for a role or opportunity that you could have wanted yourself?

**Failure mode questions**:
- What's the most common way you see engineering managers use "development" as a way to avoid having hard conversations?
- If I asked your best manager to describe what they learned from you in the last 6 months, what would they say? Be specific.

## Friendship / Mutual Betterment
**Source**: winning-with-people-095

**Core principle**: Real friendship makes both people better. Henry Ford's test: your best friend is the one who brings out the best in you.

**Belief questions**:
- Who in your professional life brings out the best in you? What do they do differently than everyone else?
- Who do you bring out the best in? How do you know?

**Practice questions**:
- Your closest professional relationships — are they accelerating you, holding you back, or neutral? How do you decide?
- When was the last time a peer gave you difficult feedback that you genuinely took to heart? What made them able to do that?

**Failure mode questions**:
- Professional friendships can become echo chambers. How do you know if your closest peers are actually challenging you vs. just confirming your worldview?
- What's the difference between a peer who is genuinely your friend and one who is your friend because you have power?

## Generosity / Giving First
**Source**: winning-with-people-090

**Core principle**: Give first, without calculating the return. The most generous are the most connected.

**Belief questions**:
- When was the last time you gave something with no expectation of return — not even goodwill? What did it cost you and what came back?
- Do you believe that generosity in leadership actually compounds? Or is that something people say because it sounds right?

**Practice questions**:
- How do you decide what to give someone in a given week — your time, attention, political capital, something else? Is that decision conscious?
- You have significant technical credibility (ML background, Meta, ServiceNow). How do you give that away without feeling like you're creating dependency?

**Failure mode questions**:
- There's a failure mode where leaders give advice as a form of control — they feel generous but actually they're just extending their influence. Have you caught yourself doing this?
- How do you know the difference between generous giving and strategic reciprocity that just looks generous?

## Celebrating Others' Thunder
**Source**: winning-with-people-085

**Core principle**: Never steal someone's thunder. When a colleague achieves something you've already done, celebrate at their level — not yours.

**Belief questions**:
- When someone on your team gets recognition for something you've done before, what's your genuine internal reaction — before you perform the right response?
- Can you think of a time you accidentally stole someone's thunder without realizing it at the time?

**Practice questions**:
- Describe how you currently celebrate others' wins. Does your public response match your private reaction?
- When someone gets credit for something you built the foundation for, how do you handle the attribution question?

**Failure mode questions**:
- Leaders often "add context" to someone else's win — "yes, great work, and here's what it really required." Is that celebrating or stealing?
- How do you celebrate a direct report's achievement without making it about you?

## Session-Derived Questions (from May 22, 2026 inaugural session)

These questions emerged from Ratul's specific answers and are more grounded in reality than generic prompts. Use them to go deeper in future sessions.

**Skip-leveling / IC-as-identity trap**:
- When you bypass the manager and things move fast, what's the emotional reward — relief, validation, something else? Be specific.
- Your VP does it too. When you use that as justification, are you actually solving a time problem — or protecting your sense of being "close to the ground"?
- How often does the manager find out you've gone direct? When they don't push back — is that health or silence?

**Career conversations / systems problem**:
- What's one specific career conversation you didn't have that you should have? Who paid the price?
- You've built a team that runs on your urgency. What would have to be true for career development to happen without you personally driving it?

**Feedback & follow-through**:
- You give direct feedback to tenured engineers and they don't push back. Have they actually absorbed it — or just learned that dissent costs more than compliance?
- When's the last time you followed up three weeks later to see if feedback actually landed? What did you find?

**The "many things to care about" trap**:
- You named this phrase three times in one session. Is that a scheduling problem — or have you built a system where the urgent automatically wins over the important?
- Who on your team is carrying the cost of your "there are always so many things" without you knowing it?

**Studying leaders by their removals**:
- You named Jonathan (process, ownership, saying no). But what did he stop doing? If you can't answer that, you haven't really studied him.

## Learnable Relational Skills / Intentional Development
**Source**: winning-with-people-103

**Core principle**: Relational success is a learnable skill, not a natural gift. The decision to develop relational capacity is available to anyone — the cost is only intention and effort.

**Belief questions** (what he actually believes vs. what he says):
- Do you believe relational skills are something you either have or you don't? Where did that belief come from — and what has it cost you?
- When you meet someone naturally charismatic, what's your internal story about why they have that and you don't?
- What's the difference between "I'm just not good with people" and "I haven't decided to be good with people yet"?

**Practice questions** (how he actually operates):
- Think about a relational skill you have now that you didn't have three years ago. What did you actually do to develop it — not a course, not a book, but the specific practice?
- Describe your approach to building a new professional relationship — not a casual connection, but someone you need to actually work with deeply. What's your process?
- When you meet someone new in a work context, what's your default posture: curious about them, or waiting to establish your own value?

**Failure mode questions** (tension/gaps):
- "Relational skills" can become an excuse for manipulation. Where's the line between "I'm investing in this relationship" and "I'm working this person"?
- The belief that relational skill is innate is most comfortable for people who already have network effects. What have you missed out on because you treated connection as talent rather than practice?
- Senior technical leaders often think expertise earns trust. What happens when expertise isn't enough — and how do you build the relational layer that expertise alone can't create?

## Complementary Strengths / Mutual Leverage
**Source**: winning-with-people-099

**Core principle**: Best partnerships are built on complementary strengths, not identical ones. Complementarity creates what neither could alone.

**Belief questions** (what he actually believes vs. what he says):
- Who in your professional life is strong where you are weak — genuinely, not just politely described that way?
- When you form a new partnership (project lead, peer, cross-functional), do you first ask "what do we both bring?" or do you assume you already know?
- What's harder for you to admit as a gap: something you're bad at, or something you need someone else to be good at for you to succeed?

**Practice questions** (how he actually operates):
- Think about a partnership where the two of you were strong in the same areas. What did you accidentally build together that either of you would have built alone?
- Describe a time you deliberately sought out someone whose strengths were the inverse of yours. What did that require you to admit about yourself?
- When you're staffing a project or forming a team, do you naturally reach for people who think like you — or do you actively weight complementary thinking styles?

**Failure mode questions** (tension/gaps):
- "Complementary" can become "I do the thinking, you do the executing." How do you make sure complementary actually means mutual leverage, not just delegation with better branding?
- Senior leaders who built their career on being the smartest person in the room struggle to form true partnerships. What's your version of needing to be the one with the answer?
- Complementarity only works if both people can name what they bring and what they lack. Can you articulate your top two gaps as clearly as your top two strengths?
- The failure mode of seeking only complementary partners is that you never build shared context — the relationship stays transactional. How do you build depth alongside complementarity?
