# Sample Coaching Session — 2026-05-21

Real session. Tips used: 104 (Self-Awareness) and 80 (Patience as Active Choice). Questions asked: 7. Format: one at a time, feedback after all.

---

## Q1 — Pattern Self-Correction

**Question:** "You lead engineering managers who themselves manage engineers. Think about the patterns you notice in your own behavior — the ones you catch yourself doing repeatedly and actively try to correct. What's one such pattern, and why do you think you have that tendency in the first place?"

**Answer:** "Sometimes, I feel the urge to give instructions to my skip reports directly because we are often under time pressure and going through the manager would typically increase the time. It's a bit top down, I've seen my VP doing the same thing."

**Follow-up pushed to:** "When was the last time you got surprised by how someone responded to you? What did you learn about your lens?"

**Coach's note:** The answer revealed the identity-protection loop (validation = still close to the ground). Not a scheduling problem — a relevance problem.

---

## Q2 — Real Driver Behind Skip-Leveling

**Question:** "When you do it, what's actually happening in that moment? Is it genuinely time pressure, or is it sometimes that you trust your own judgment more than your manager's — or even enjoy the direct hit of speed and competence? What's the real driver for you?"

**Answer:** "Hmm, yeah it's a mix of all of these. But if you ask for the real driver it'll still be the time pressure."

**Follow-up:** "You mentioned you sometimes enjoy the direct hit of speed and competence. When that kick in kicks in — what's the emotional reward? Validation that you're still close to the ground?"

**Answer:** "Validation that you're still close to the ground."

**Coach's note:** He named it. Most leaders don't. This is the core reveal of the session.

---

## Q3 — Bypassing the Manager

**Question:** "When you bypass your manager and go direct — how often does the manager find out? And when they do, what's the typical fallout?"

**Answer:** "Often either I or the engineer loop him in afterwards. I honestly don't know what happens in other cases. But I've never sensed any frustration from him regarding this."

**Coach's note:** Gap in visibility. Manager not complaining ≠ system healthy. The coach didn't challenge further here — the pattern would surface again later.

---

## Q4 — Career Conversations

**Question:** "Think of a specific time when you were genuinely impatient with someone on your team, not because they were wrong, but because you felt the clock pressure. What did you sacrifice in that moment by not slowing down?"

**Answer:** "I've often found myself guilty of ignoring the career development discussion with my reports because there are always so much to do and status updates to ask for. These important discussions frequently take a back seat."

**Push for specifics:** "What specifically was sacrificed?"

**Answer:** "I missed setting expectations for a given eng level clearly, it creates risk for the end of year performance evaluation."

**Coach's note:** Concrete. The coach didn't let him stay at the pattern level — extracted the specific downstream cost.

---

## Q5 — Patience Calibration

**Question:** "How do you actually calibrate patience differently for the two groups (tenured AI folks vs. stronger on SWE side)?"

**Answer:** "I assign tasks depending on their background, skillset and seniority."

**Coach's note:** This answered task allocation, not patience calibration. The coach reframed: "When a strong software engineer who just joined the AI space makes a naive architecture choice, do you correct fast or let them reason through? And the same for a deep ML person writing messy code — same patience?"

**Follow-up answer:** "For a new one I try to reason through and provide sufficient context. For a tenured person I try to give direct feedback."

**Coach's note:** Inverted from typical — more patience for new people, direct for tenured. Interesting.

---

## Q6 — Follow-Up on Feedback

**Question:** "When you give direct feedback to a tenured person and they just say 'sure, makes sense' — do you ever follow up to see if the thing actually changed?"

**Answer:** "I don't always get the opportunity to follow up (because there are often too many things to care about)"

**Coach's note:** "I don't get the opportunity" is a dodge. The coach named it: "That's not a scheduling problem. That's a system design problem. 'Too many things to care about' is a symptom, not a cause."

---

## Q7 — The Model Leader

**Question:** "When you imagine a leader who gets both speed AND deep team development — who doesn't trade one for the other — who comes to mind? It can be someone you've worked for, worked with, or read about."

**Answer:** "Yes, I've seen such manager at Meta (where I used to work). Jonathan. He used to focus on developing processes, setting up owners for different things, and was good at saying no."

**Follow-up:** "What did Jonathan NOT do that you find yourself doing constantly?"

**Answer:** "I don't know"

**Coach's note:** Can't name the removals — never studied them. Builder vs. remover mindset gap.

---

## Full Feedback Given

**Verdict:** "You are an IC with management responsibilities, not yet a manager of managers."

**Key themes named:**
1. Identity-Protection Loop
2. Invisible Systemic Debt
3. The Urgency Ratchet
4. Polite Team vs. Trusting Team
5. Builder vs. Remover Mindset

**Risks named:**
| Pattern | Risk |
|---------|------|
| Skip-leveling | Manager becomes irrelevant; engineers route around chain |
| No career conversations | Performance review surprise; team stops believing you have backs |
| No follow-up | Compliance without behavior change; no signal before visible |
| Urgency-driven system | Important-but-not-urgent doesn't happen until broken |
| No removal practice | Calendar fills; never designs what to stop doing |

**One concrete action assigned:** Have a single career conversation with one engineer. "What do you want to be doing in two years, and what's in your way right now?" No prep, no agenda, just listen.

---

## What Worked in This Session

- Pushed for specific moments vs. generic patterns — Q4 became real because of this
- Named the identity-protection loop when he revealed it in Q1/Q2
- "I don't get the opportunity" called as a dodge, not accepted
- Q7 follow-up on Jonathan's *removals* was the sharpest question of the session — he genuinely couldn't answer, which was the answer
- Mid-session synthesis delivered before Q5 ("Everything you've described traces back to the same root") — Ratul confirmed it landed

## What Would Change Next Time

- When Q3's answer revealed the manager not complaining, could have pushed harder on what that silence costs — the pattern of "I don't know what happens" is itself the data point
- Q5 patience calibration answer was incomplete — the follow-up to that question didn't get a full answer because Ratul pivoted to describing his team composition rather than his emotional reaction when corrected
